# Five Guys Operational Playbook and General Manager Screening

## Summary

An operational analysis of Five Guys' expansion from a single family-operated Virginia concept (17 years at 5 units) to a $2B global empire. The framework details four core operational rules: real-estate/search capture over paid media, disciplined single-page menu engineering (28%–32% COGS on premium inputs), the unit economics of premium fast food, and a 5-question interview framework for screening high-ownership store General Managers.

---

## 1. Zero Paid Media and Unbranded Search Capture

While enterprise quick-service burger chains spend hundreds of millions annually on media (e.g., McDonald's spending $\$2\text{M}–\$3\text{M}/\text{day}$ on national advertising), Five Guys allocates **$0 to broadcast TV, radio, or billboard advertising**.

### The Real Estate & SEO Principle
1. **Premium Real Estate over Ad Arbitrage**: Founder Jerry Murrell established that paying higher commercial lease rates for sites with proven organic foot-traffic density is structurally cheaper than leasing low-cost real estate and attempting to generate foot traffic via paid advertising.
2. **Unbranded High-Intent Local Search**:
   - $85\%$ of the search queries driving organic discovery to Five Guys locations are **unbranded generic terms** (e.g., *"burger near me"*, *"fresh fries near me"*, *"lunch near me"*).
   - Capturing top-3 map pack positions for unbranded local search intercepts diners at the moment of intent within the 3-mile trade radius.

## 2. General Manager Hiring: The 5 Diagnostic Questions

Treating store managers like operating partners requires screening for extreme operational ownership, systemic thinking, and cost literacy.

### The 5 Operational Interview Questions (Inga Stanfield Rubric)

| Question | Diagnostic Objective | Ideal Response Signal | Disqualifying Red Flag |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. "Tell me about a problem that wasn't your fault, but you fixed it anyway."** | Ownership vs. Blame attribution | Describes immediate operational resolution; zero focus on whose fault it was. | Spends interview time detailing who made the error or venting frustration. |
| **2. "What was broken in every restaurant you've worked in, and why does nobody fix it?"** | Systemic pattern recognition | Identifies systemic SOP gaps (e.g., prep cadence, communication logs) and explains how they corrected them. | Complains vaguely about lazy coworkers or bad corporate management. |
| **3. "What was the first thing you did the last time your shift fell apart?"** | Composure & team leverage | Assesses line bottlenecks, reprioritizes station labor, and coaches the team through the surge. | Attempts to "hero" the shift by doing all prep/cooking themselves while the team watches. |
| **4. "What was your last restaurant's food cost % and weekly purchase spend off the top of your head?"** | Direct financial literacy | Immediately recites exact percentage (e.g., $31.2\%$) and average weekly purchase dollars. | Hesitates or admits they only pressed buttons on automated corporate POS software. |
| **5. "What kind of owner brings the worst out of you?"** | Cultural self-awareness & compatibility | Gives a calm, nuanced, honest assessment of management style friction. | States *"I get along with everyone"* (defensive evasion) or displays active resentment toward accountability. |

## 3. Disciplined Single-Page Menu Engineering

Five Guys resisted decades of franchise, consumer, and industry pressure to introduce chicken sandwiches, green salads, drive-thrus, or breakfast items.

### The True Cost of Menu Item Additions
Operators mistakenly evaluate new menu introductions based solely on raw ingredient cost and selling price. Five Guys accounts for systemic operational friction:
- **Dedicated Equipment Footprint**: Adding fried chicken requires separate dedicated fryers to prevent allergen and flavor cross-contamination with peanut-oil fries.
- **Workflow & Line Cadence**: Non-standard preparation flows create kitchen bottlenecks, increasing ticket load times for core burger orders.
- **Supply Chain Leverage**: Concentrating purchasing volume across a minimal number of raw ingredients (fresh beef, Idaho potatoes, peanut oil, cheese, bacon, buns) unlocks massive volume discounts.
- **Benchmark Performance**: Maintains **28% to 32% COGS** (below the burger industry benchmark of $30\%–35\%$) despite utilizing 100% fresh, never-frozen beef and hand-cut potatoes.

## 4. "Spend More to Make More" Unit Economics

### Premium Fast Food Margin Geometry
- Fresh ground chuck costs approximately $15\%–20\%$ more than frozen commercial patties, and hand-washing, slicing, and double-frying raw potatoes increases back-of-house prep labor.
- **Pricing Elasticity**: Transparent, high-quality ingredient execution allowed Five Guys to establish the "Better Burger" fast-casual category, charging $\$12.00–\$15.00$ for a burger and fry combination.

$$\text{Commodity Fast Food Ticket: } \$8.50 \text{ at } 12\% \text{ Net Margin} = \$1.02 \text{ Net Profit}$$
$$\text{Premium Fast Casual Ticket: } \$14.00 \text{ at } 15\% \text{ Net Margin} = \$2.10 \text{ Net Profit}$$

Even with higher input costs and labor, higher ticket sizes generate **more than double the net cash dollars per guest transaction**.

### The Single Quality Anchor
To sustain organic word-of-mouth without paid advertising, an independent restaurant must dominate at least one unmistakable sensory quality signal:
- Examples: In-house hand-ground chuck, scratch-baked brioche buns, fresh hand-shredded cheese, or proprietary house-made dipping sauces.
- *Core Strategic Question*: *"If your best regular had to explain to a friend why your food is worth paying 30% more, what exact quality signal would they cite?"*
